A life in ten frames · 1828–1910

Tolstoy

Count Lev Nikolayevich Tolstoy · Лев Николаевич Толстой

He was born owning people. He lost his birthplace at cards, survived a siege, and wrote two of the biggest novels ever made. Then he decided novels were not the point, and spent thirty years trying to give everything away.

1828

Born into a country where people were property

Lev Nikolayevich Tolstoy was born on 9 September 1828 at Yasnaya Polyana, “Bright Glade,” his family’s estate about 200 km south of Moscow. He was a count. The land came with the people who worked it.

These were serfs. They could not leave, could be sold, and could be flogged. By the last count before they were freed, more than one Russian in three belonged to a landowner.

His mother died before he turned two. His father died when he was eight. Aunts raised him, in a house run by serfs.

Meanwhile

Pushkin is 29 and the most famous poet in Russia. There is not one railway in the country. Tsar Nicholas I is three years into a reign that began by crushing the Decembrist revolt.

1847–54

The heir who gambled away the house he was born in

At 16 he went to Kazan University. He failed exams, switched from Oriental languages to law, and quit in 1847 without a degree. That spring, at 18, he started a diary. He kept it for 63 years.

The same year he inherited Yasnaya Polyana and its serfs. He tried to improve their lives. They did not trust him, and he gave up. So he went to Moscow and Petersburg to drink, chase women and play cards.

In 1854 the card debts came due. He sold the main house, the one he was born in. The buyer took it apart and rebuilt it on his own estate. Tolstoy lived the rest of his life in one of the two small wings that were left.

Meanwhile

1848: revolutions sweep Europe, and Nicholas I tightens censorship at home. 1849: a young writer named Dostoevsky is sentenced to death, then reprieved on the scaffold at the last moment.

1851–57

Soldier, war reporter, celebrity

In 1851, bored and in debt, he followed his brother Nikolai to the Caucasus, where Russia was fighting a long war against the mountain peoples. Between raids he wrote a story called Childhood. A Petersburg magazine printed it in 1852, signed only “L. N.” Readers wanted to know who L. N. was.

Then came the Crimean War. Britain, France and the Ottoman Empire laid siege to the naval base at Sevastopol for 349 days. Tolstoy served in a battery on the Fourth Bastion, the most dangerous spot in the city, and wrote sketches from inside the siege: no glory, just fear, mud and bodies.

Back in Petersburg he was a star, and quarreled with nearly everyone. In 1857 he went west. In Paris he watched a man guillotined, and wrote that he would never serve any government again.

Meanwhile

Russia loses the war in 1856. Its serf army, carrying muskets against rifles, is outclassed. The new tsar, Alexander II, concludes that the old order has to change.

1861

Freedom, with a bill attached

On 3 March 1861, Alexander II freed the serfs: about 23 million people, more than five times the number enslaved in the United States at the time.

But freedom came with a bill. Peasants got less land than they had farmed, and had to pay for it over 49 years. The payments were cancelled only in 1907, after a revolution forced the tsar’s hand.

Tolstoy, home for good, served as a local “peace arbitrator” in land disputes. He sided with peasants so often that landowners petitioned to remove him. He also ran a free school for peasant children, with no grades and no punishments.

Meanwhile

1861 is also the year the American Civil War begins. In July 1862 the tsar’s police search Tolstoy’s school for a secret printing press. They find none.

1862

Sophia, his first reader

In the autumn of 1862 he married Sophia Behrs, a court doctor’s daughter. He was 34. She was 18. Before the wedding he made her read his diaries, the gambling and the women included. She never forgot it.

She gave birth to 13 children in 25 years. Five died young. She ran the estate, handled his publishing, and copied his drafts by hand at night, often the only person who could read his writing. Family legend says she copied War and Peace seven times.

Meanwhile

1862: Turgenev publishes Fathers and Sons. A year earlier Tolstoy had challenged him to a duel after a quarrel over lunch. It never happened, but they did not make peace for 17 years.

1863–69

War and Peace: 1812, told fifty years later

With a wife, a home and money coming in, he began a novel about the Decembrists. To explain them he kept going back in time, until he reached Napoleon. It became War and Peace: four volumes and an epilogue, from Petersburg ballrooms to the field at Borodino.

Its hero is not Napoleon. It is history itself, and the millions of small choices no general controls. Great men, Tolstoy argued, only think they steer events.

The year the novel is finished, Mendeleev publishes the periodic table, the Suez Canal opens, and in Paris a retired engineer, Charles Minard, draws the chart below of the very same war.

1873–77

Anna Karenina and the railway

Happy families are all alike; every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way.

Opening line · tr. Constance Garnett, 1901

Anna Karenina ran in a Moscow magazine from 1875 to 1877. A society woman leaves her husband for an officer, and pays for it with everything. The railway runs through the whole book, new, fast and dangerous. Anna meets her lover at a station, and dies under a train.

The editor refused to print the final part, because it mocked Russia’s volunteers for the war in Serbia. Tolstoy published it as a separate book.

Meanwhile

April 1877: Russia goes to war with the Ottoman Empire again, its third war with the Turks in Tolstoy’s lifetime.

1879–82

Everything, and no reason to live

At fifty Tolstoy had what anyone could want. Then a question stopped him cold, and would not leave: what is the point?

In A Confession he describes hiding a cord so he would not hang himself from the crossbeam in his room, and giving up hunting so he would not be tempted to turn the gun on himself.

He looked for an answer in science, philosophy and the Orthodox Church. He found it, he decided, in the Sermon on the Mount, read literally: love your enemies, do not resist evil with force, own nothing.

Meanwhile

March 1881: revolutionaries kill Alexander II with a bomb. Tolstoy writes to the new tsar, Alexander III, begging him to forgive the killers. Five are hanged in April.

1882–1908

The count in peasant boots

He dressed like a peasant, ploughed, and made his own boots. He walked from Moscow to Yasnaya Polyana, about 200 km, more than once. He signed his estates over to his family and gave up the copyright on everything he wrote after 1881. Sophia, left to keep a large household afloat, fought him over it for the rest of their marriage.

His new books attacked the state, the army and the Church. The censors banned them; they spread anyway, copied by hand and printed abroad. In 1901 the Holy Synod excommunicated him. That year the publisher Aleksey Suvorin wrote in his diary that Russia had two tsars: Nicholas II and Leo Tolstoy.

Meanwhile

1891–92: famine on the Volga; Tolstoy and his daughters run hundreds of free kitchens. 1905: revolution. 1908: he records his voice on an Edison phonograph, and Prokudin-Gorsky takes his portrait, one of the earliest color photographs made in Russia.

1910

Ten days in November

Home had become a war. Sophia and his disciple Vladimir Chertkov fought over his diaries, his copyrights and his will. In the summer of 1910 he signed a secret will in the woods, leaving his work to the public.

Before dawn on 10 November, aged 82, he slipped out with his doctor. His note to Sophia said he was doing what old men often do: leaving worldly life to spend his last days in quiet.

He caught pneumonia on the train. At the small junction of Astapovo he was carried into the stationmaster’s house. Reporters, film crews and police filled the platform. Sophia was kept outside until he was unconscious.

After

A grave with no name

When they were boys, his brother Nikolai told him the secret of how to make all people happy was written on a green stick, buried at the edge of a ravine in the woods at Yasnaya Polyana. Tolstoy asked to be buried on that spot.

He was. There is no cross, no stone, no name, just a low mound of grass under the trees. The station at Astapovo now carries his name. His collected works fill 90 volumes.

Notes on dates and numbers

Dates are New Style. Russia kept the Julian calendar until 1918, 12 days behind the West in the 1800s and 13 after 1900. By the Russian calendar Tolstoy was born on 28 August and died on 7 November.

Serf figures come from the tenth tax census, 1857–58, for the 50 provinces of European Russia. Army sizes and temperatures follow Minard’s 1869 chart, simplified. The estate plan is schematic; the maps are drawn to scale.
